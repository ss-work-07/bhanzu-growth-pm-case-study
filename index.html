Executive summary

About 35 of every 100 families who book a free demo do not come, and a family that misses is almost never recovered. Most of those families booked for today or tomorrow, so the fix has to work on a short gap, not only a long one.

3 findings
  1. The drop is a step, not a slope. Attendance holds at 66% for 0-1 days, slips to 62-64% at 2-3 days, and sits at 55-57% from day 4. Day 6 (63%) is likely noise or a weekday effect.
  2. Most no-shows are near-term bookings. The 65% benchmark sits 1 point below the 0-1 day rate, so about three quarters of bookings must land within 48 hours. That puts about 25 of every 35 no-shows in the 0-1 day group. Fixing every longer gap adds about 1.25 points at best.
  3. A 5-day booker is a different parent, and a same-day booker still misses 34%. Selection and fading intent explain the long-gap drop. Forgetting cannot explain the same-day misses: nobody notices a skip, second thoughts about a sales call, and a child who was never asked.
Solution

Ship a rewritten, capped WhatsApp sequence and one-tap rescheduling first: cheap, live in days, and they reach every booking. Then the bet: a 5-minute warm-up quest for the child, linked in the booking confirmation. I chose it because it is the only idea aimed at the child, who is the one attending; it works on a booking made at noon for 5 PM; and it hands the teacher and the counsellor something real to open with.

Early signal

The morning-of "Yes, we'll be there" WhatsApp reply rate, test arm vs control, counting only the WhatsApp reply. Readable by day 3.

Decision

Go/no-go on attendance per booking (+3 points, about 4,000 bookings per arm). Enrollments per booking confirms it (about 35,000 per arm) and must not fall.

First 2 weeks
  • Week 1: pull booking-level data; call 10 families who missed and 5 who came; get templates approved; launch the new sequence and reschedule flow against the current one.
  • Week 2: pilot the quest with 10 teachers; fix the biggest drop in the handover funnel; start the randomised quest test.
The ask

A booking-level data export, counsellor time to reach 15 families, WhatsApp template approval, and one engineer for 2 to 3 weeks with 10 teachers for the pilot.

1.Read the data

Part 1. Read the data

The brief: what pattern stands out, what questions it raises, what I'd want to know before drawing conclusions.

My read

The gap matters, but it starts late and it is small. The bigger problem sits in bookings for today and tomorrow, which are most bookings and most no-shows.

What stands out

  • Flat, then a step. 0-1 days: 66%. 2-3 days: 62-64%. 4-5 days: 57%. That is a threshold around day 4, not a fixed cost for each day of waiting.
  • Day 6 breaks the pattern. 63% at 6 days, then the low of 55% at 7. A real effect rarely bounces like that. Either the rows are small, or the weekday of booking and session is mixed into "days gap".
  • The benchmark gives away the booking mix. 65% overall is barely below the 66% near-term rate. Long gaps can only pull the average down a little, so few bookings can be long gaps. The arithmetic is below.
Booking gapShare of bookings (est.)AttendedNo-shows per 100 bookings
0-1 days7566%25.5

All10064.75% ≈ 65%35.3
  • Assumptions: rows within a bucket count equally (2-3 days = 63%, 4-7 days = 58%); there are 3 bookings at 2-3 days for every 2 at 4-7 days; nothing beyond 7 days is missing from the table.
  • The mix is not unique. The benchmark is rounded, so anything from 64.5% to 65.5% fits. Under the 3:2 assumption that means 70% to 90% of bookings are 0-1 days out. 75/15/10 is a round mix inside that range. Every mix in it leaves the 0-1 day group with most no-shows.
  • What each fix is worth. Raising every 4-7 day booking to 66% adds 0.8 points overall (10 × 8 ÷ 100). Raising every 2-7 day booking to 66% adds about 1.25 (15 × 3 + 10 × 8, ÷ 100). Cutting 0-1 day no-shows by a fifth, 34% to 27%, adds about 5 (75 × 6.8 ÷ 100 = 5.1).

→ The gap is real but worth about a point. The prize is in near-term bookings, so any fix must work for them too.

What it raises

  • Why were days 4, 5 and 7 flagged: a test of significance, or a visual cut? With rows this size, the flags may be noise.
  • Is "days gap" a cause, or a label for a different kind of parent, channel or weekday?
  • Why does a same-day booking still lose 34%? Forgetting cannot explain that.

2.Diagnose

Part 2. Diagnose the drop

The brief: do I agree with the two beliefs; what happens between booking and session; what forces work against attendance as time passes.

Belief 1"Some on our team believe attendance falls as the booking gap grows."
Partly agree

Attendance does fall with the gap, but as a step after day 3, not a steady decline. And it explains a minority of no-shows: about 10 of every 35. If the team treats the gap as the main cause, it will optimise for the 25% of bookings where the smaller share of the problem sits.

Belief 2"They also believe a parent who books 5 days out is no different from one who books the same day, since both wanted the demo when they clicked the ad."
Disagree

Clicking the ad was the last moment they were alike. Three things separate them:

  • They chose differently. Picking a slot 5 days out when today's is open often signals lower urgency. It can also mean a real constraint, like a working parent who only has Saturdays.
  • Their reason fades. A booking starts with a specific feeling: a bad test, a homework fight, a reel of a child doing sums at speed. That feeling peaks at the click.
  • Life gets more chances. Each extra day adds a tuition class, a birthday, a fever or a school project.

How I'd separate selection from decay

The two need different fixes. If it is selection, pulling parents into earlier slots will not help. If it is decay, more near-term capacity is a real lever.

MethodCompareReading
Natural experiment (existing data)Parents who picked a far slot while near ones were open vs parents pushed out because near slots were fullPushed-out parents attend like same-day bookers: selection. They drop too: decay.
Randomised nudge (idea D)Families randomly shown near slots first vs the current pageNudged families attend like natural near-term bookers: decay. They attend like far bookers: selection.

A. Rewritten WhatsApp sequence

Now
What
Each message gives a reason to come (a named teacher who has prepared), makes the plan concrete, asks for a small yes, and defuses the fear of a sales call. Tappable options are WhatsApp quick-reply buttons.
Why
It reaches every booking, including the same-day ones where most no-shows are, and it ships in days.
Lever
Reciprocity, implementation intentions, a small public commitment, lower anticipated sales pressure.

Volume and cost guardrails

  • Cap: at most one message we start per day, except on class day. Replies to the parent don't count.
  • Templates: anything outside WhatsApp's 24-hour reply window needs a pre-approved template. Logistics messages are utility templates; the puzzles are marketing templates.
  • Cost: utility messages cost a fraction of a rupee each in India, marketing messages around a rupee (check the current Meta rate card). Even 8 messages is a few rupees per booking, small next to the ad cost of the booking.
  • Hard stop: block and opt-out rate. A rising block rate lowers the account's quality rating, which can limit messaging for every family.

What I'd ship first and why

Order

A and B now, C next, D when capacity allows, E to G only with evidence.

  • A and B first. They are cheap, reach every family including same-day bookings, and can go live in a week as a test against the current sequence.
  • C is the bigger bet, and its link rides in A's first message, so the two compound. It needs A in place, which is why it follows.
  • D only works if near-term slots exist; it waits on a capacity check.
  • E to G cost people, money or trust, so they wait for evidence from A to C.
Week 0: talk to families before building

Call 10 families who missed a demo in the last fortnight and 5 who came. Ten minutes each, four questions:

  1. What were you doing at the time the class started?
  2. When did you last think about the class before that?
  3. What did your child know about it?
  4. What did you expect would happen after the class?

Every force in Part 2 is a hypothesis. These calls tell me which are real before we spend build time.

4.Build

Part 4. The micro-experience: Math Warm-up Quest

The brief: build the one idea that does most to lift attendance and carry engagement into the demo, counsellor call and enrollment; say why this idea, what the parent and child see and do, and the one early signal in the first 3 days.

What it is and how to try it

A 5-minute quest the parent opens from the booking confirmation and hands to the child. It ends with a locked level the teacher opens live and a stamp with the child's name, then gives the parent a short report and a readiness check. The teacher and counsellor each get a brief. Both links below are real and usable. They run on free servers, so the first load can take up to a minute to wake.

Warm-up quest. The page a parent opens from WhatsApp. Best on a phone. "Change booking details" at the top lets you try another name, age group or gap. The Teacher and Counsellor tabs show what each role sees.

Warm-up quest ↗

Full prototype and phone app. The quest inside the whole journey: booking page, the WhatsApp sequence on a simulated phone with working quick replies, a time control to skip to class day, the teacher and counsellor console, and the experiment readout. The phone app is the same loop built for a phone, with Parent, Child and Staff modes. Parents abroad see every time in their own time zone.

Full prototype ↗ Phone app ↗

Why this idea over the others

  • It is the only idea aimed at the child, who is the one attending. Messages persuade the parent. A child who wants the locked level reminds the parent at 4:55 PM.
  • It works on a short gap. It sits in the first message and takes 5 minutes, so a parent who books at noon for 5 PM can still do it. That matters because most no-shows are near-term.
  • It counters the forces that apply on day 0. A teacher who has seen the stamp means someone expects the child. The child has been asked. The report and checklist make the plan concrete.
  • It carries through the funnel. The teacher and counsellor open with what the child and parent actually did and said.
  • The trade-off I accept: it takes longer to build than A or B, and some parents won't hand over the phone. A and B ship first so the quest is additive, not a dependency.

What the parent and child see and do

  1. ParentTaps "Start the warm-up" in the confirmation. Sees the date, time in their own time zone and teacher, answers what would you most like help with?, and gives consent: only the child's first name is used, and only the teacher and counsellor see the answers.
  2. ParentIf the child isn't around, taps "isn't with me right now" and picks when to be reminded (4 PM, 7 PM, tomorrow morning), or copies the link to the child's tablet.
  3. ParentWhen the child is there: "Hand over the phone".
  4. ChildChooses English or हिंदी and picks a secret maths sign (π, ∞, √).
  5. ChildPlays 5 puzzles for their age group (6-8, 9-11, 12-14), written in rupees and laddoos. Every answer reveals a Bhanzu trick, and correct answers get the teacher's red tick. Wrong answers get a hint, not a cross. 6-8 year olds can have puzzles read aloud.
  6. ChildFinds a locked level the teacher opens live in class, and picks what they want to get good at.
  7. ChildEarns a stamp with their name and their teacher's, then hands the phone back.
  8. ParentReads what the child did (never how fast), how the hour will run minute by minute, one note about the counsellor call, and who started Bhanzu.
  9. ParentDoes a 30-second checklist: Google Calendar (a real link), which screen, who sits in, a real check of this phone's connection and screen. Then "We'll be there", or the nearest new slots.
  10. ParentFor gaps of 3+ days, sees the puzzle schedule up to class day and can try tomorrow's puzzle.
  11. TeacherSees results by skill (with times, which the parent never sees), the child's goal, what the parent mentioned, and a script for the first 3 minutes.
  12. CounsellorSees confirmation status, setup, a no-show risk level with the action to take, and an opening line built from the parent's and child's own words. The teacher doesn't see the risk level.

The one early signal

The one early signal, first 3 days

Morning-of "Yes, we'll be there" WhatsApp reply rate, test arm vs control.

  • Why it works: both arms get the same morning message, and booking is randomised, so a gap between arms is caused by the quest.
  • Only the WhatsApp reply counts. The quest has its own "We'll be there" button that control families never see. Counting it would flatter the test arm.
  • When it reads: by day 3, from bookings made 1-2 days ahead. Same-day bookings made after 9 AM don't get the morning message, so they wait for attendance.
  • How I read it: test above control means keep filling the test. At or below control means the quest isn't building commitment: check the supporting funnel before adding traffic. In the control arm, I first confirm that a YES predicts attendance before trusting it.
Supporting check, not a success measure

Share of booked families whose child reaches the stamp within 24 hours.

40% or more: fine. 20-40%: fix the step with the biggest drop (opened, handed over, started, finished). Under 20%: rethink the handover. Completion alone can't justify scaling: families who finish were probably more likely to attend anyway.

Prototype limits: the calendar link and device check are real; the class link and WhatsApp sends are simulated in the full prototype and shown, not sent, in the quest. Parent screens are English only for now, child screens English and Hindi.

Risks

  • Message fatigue. More messages could raise blocks and hurt every family's messaging. The cap and the block-rate kill criterion handle it.
  • The quest becomes a test. A child who struggles could arrive anxious. Hints instead of crosses, and no speed shown to the parent.
  • Teacher load. If teachers ignore the brief, the carry-over into the demo is lost. The pilot measures it before scale.
  • Slow confirmation. Enrollments per booking may take months at Bhanzu's volume. I'd decide on attendance and keep the split running rather than wait.
  • Abroad families. Slots run on India teacher hours. Showing local times fixes confusion, not availability; US and UK families may still lack a sensible slot.

What I'd do differently with real data

  • Replace the back-solved mix with real row counts and redo the no-show split.
  • Re-cut attendance by weekday, channel, time zone and month before trusting any gap effect.
  • Set the minimum detectable lift from real volume, and size the test on it rather than on +3 points.
  • Fit the risk level on past no-shows instead of a rule of thumb.
  • Put real parent quotes from the Week 0 calls next to each force in Part 2.
